02 - The biggest nugget

The dilution pause

One sentence in the PR matters more than the headline: no new shares printed on the way to closing.

“Safe Harbor does not intend to draw on its existing equity line of credit with CREO Capital until the closing of the Proposed Transaction.”

PR, Key Terms (verbatim)

NO MORE DILUTION until closing. In plain terms: Safe Harbor plans to reach the $1 Nasdaq minimum by completing the merger, not by printing new shares on its dilution line.

Not by a reverse split. A split is a last resort only if the merger path does not get the price to $1.

Show the details

Why holders should care:

  • Supply stops growing into catalysts. Definitive agreement, Opifex financials, and the fairness opinion can land without freshly printed shares hitting the bid.
  • It protects the 15% math. Every share not printed pre-close is value that stays with existing holders instead of being spread thinner.
  • It is a signal, not just mechanics. A company planning to dilute its way to April does not volunteer a pause.
  • Paired with Series B converting at close (see §4), the capital structure is meant to be cleaned up at closing, not drip-fed into the market before it.

Honest caveat: the PR says “does not intend to draw.” That is stated intent in a non-binding term sheet, not a contractual lock in a definitive agreement yet. Watch for the same language to survive into the definitive deal. If it does, the pause is real. If it disappears, that is a tell.

Show how the flex works

The term sheet starts at 15% for existing SHFS holders and 85% for Opifex holders, but that split adjusts to reflect SHFS’s valuation at closing, supported by a fairness opinion. In plain terms, the split is re-measured when the deal closes, not locked at the numbers in the October PR. That is why the honest answer at the $50M floor is a range, about $1.13 to $1.58 a share, not just the $1.35 midpoint.

  • If SHFS holds its value into closing, holders get more. A higher SHFS valuation at closing can push the SHFS allocation above 15%, up to 17.5%. Opifex holders then get 82.5% instead of 85%. At the $50M floor, 17.5% works out to about $1.58 a share.
  • If SHFS’s valuation falls into closing, holders get less. A lower SHFS valuation at closing can pull the allocation down to 12.5%, with Opifex holders at 87.5%. At the $50M floor, 12.5% works out to about $1.13 a share.
  • It is relative valuation at closing, not just today’s price. A good week for the stock now does not lock in 17.5%, and a bad week now does not lock in 12.5%. What counts is where SHFS’s valuation stands relative to the deal values when the transaction closes, as tested by the fairness opinion process.

Think of it as a collar that protects both sides: Opifex is protected if SHFS is worth less at closing than it looked in October, and SHFS holders are rewarded if the company holds or builds its value on the way to closing.

05 - Let’s be straight about the split

The plan is compliance through the merger. A reverse split is a last resort.

The company is currently focused on reaching Nasdaq compliance through the merger, not through a reverse split, unless absolutely necessary. A reverse split is not the plan. It is the fallback only if the merger path does not get the share price to $1.

What the PR actually says

SHFS “would seek stockholder approval of a reverse stock split to the extent needed to meet Nasdaq’s bid price requirement.” Anyone saying a split is impossible has not read that paragraph. But “to the extent needed” is the key phrase: it is conditional, not the plan.

The current focus

The focus is reaching the $1 minimum through the merger. The floor math prices at about $1.35 fully diluted, above $1 with no split doing the work. A split also does not change ownership: 15% is 15% either way.

Bottom line: a reverse split is a last resort, not the plan. The plan is to get to $1 through the merger. A split only enters the picture if that path falls short and it becomes absolutely necessary. Fear the deal failing, not the split.
Show the details

Read the order of operations in the PR: first the merger, priced at about $1.35 a share fully diluted at the $50M floor, above the $1 Nasdaq bid test. The reverse split language sits behind that, as approval the company would seek only “to the extent needed” to meet the bid price requirement.

So the honest framing is: the company is currently focused on reaching Nasdaq compliance through the merger, not through a reverse split, unless absolutely necessary. If the merger path gets the share price to $1, no split is needed. A split is a last resort if the merger path does not get the share price to $1, not the plan and not the thesis.

The same PR paragraph also contemplates a possible registered equity financing to unaffiliated investors (if necessary) and a direct investment of up to $1.0M, alongside the split approval.

08 - Risks, stated plainly

What could break this

  • It is non-binding · no assurance a definitive agreement happens
  • $50M is a floor, not an audit · the fairness opinion can reprice it
  • Opifex financials are still undisclosed · no revenue or portfolio names yet
  • Approvals can fail · Series B consents, stockholders, Nasdaq, financing
  • Closing dilution still exists · raise, $1.0M investment, 10% incentive reserve
  • SHFS is thinly traded · size moves the price
Show the details
  • It is non-binding. Except for exclusivity and confidentiality, the term sheet “does not obligate either party to complete the Proposed Transaction” and there is “no assurance a definitive agreement will be executed.” That is the #1 risk, and the PR says it itself.
  • $50M is a floor, not an audit. It is “subject to an independent valuation and fairness opinion.” If Opifex’s delivered financials do not support it, the math in §3 reprices, downward.
  • Opifex financials are still undisclosed. No revenue, headcount, or named portfolio companies in the PR. Until the 15-business-day delivery lands, Opifex is a chairman, a strategy, and a valuation claim.
  • Consents and approvals can fail. Series B holder consents, stockholder approval, Nasdaq approval, due diligence, and any required financing are all closing conditions.
  • Closing dilution still exists. The dilution pause covers the road to closing. At closing there may be a registered raise, the up-to-$1.0M direct investment, and a 10% incentive reserve, all disclosed, all dilutive to the headline share count.
  • Position and liquidity risk is personal. SHFS is thinly traded. Any large holder exiting at size will move the price against themselves; that is a trading fact, not a deal term.
